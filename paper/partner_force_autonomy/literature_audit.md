# Core literature audit

This file records the literature used to position the manuscript. It is a
source-verification record, not a substitute for the bibliography.

## Novelty boundary

The manuscript does **not** claim that dependency, weak sustainment, political
misalignment, or limited absorptive capacity are new findings. Those problems
are established in the security-assistance literature and in U.S. government
evaluation.

The manuscript's intended contribution is narrower:

1. assistance can alter the identity of the military service that binds the
   partner system;
2. short-run supported capability and long-run indigenous autonomy can move in
   opposite directions through that process;
3. indigenous development in one subsystem need not improve whole-system
   autonomy if successful development causes a different service to bind; and
4. the appropriate object of evaluation is therefore indigenous production at
   the **moving binding constraint**, not only the output of the assisted
   subsystem.

## Verified core sources

### Biddle, Macdonald, and Baker 2018

**Citation:** Stephen Biddle, Julia Macdonald, and Ryan Baker. "Small Footprint,
Small Payoff: The Military Effectiveness of Security Force Assistance."
*Journal of Strategic Studies* 41(1-2): 89-142.

**DOI:** `10.1080/01402390.2017.1307745`

**Use:** Principal-agent explanation for limited SFA effectiveness under
interest misalignment, monitoring problems, and weak enforcement.

**Novelty implication:** The present paper should not present recipient-provider
interest divergence as a new mechanism.

### Biddle 2017

**Citation:** Stephen Biddle. "Building Security Forces and Stabilizing Nations:
The Problem of Agency." *Daedalus* 146(4): 126-138.

**DOI:** `10.1162/DAED_a_00464`

**Use:** Concise theoretical statement of SFA as a principal-agent problem and
the limits of low-footprint assistance.

### Metz 2023

**Citation:** Rachel Tecott Metz. "The Cult of the Persuasive: Why U.S. Security
Assistance Fails." *International Security* 47(3): 95-135.

**DOI:** `10.1162/isec_a_00453`

**Use:** Influence and organizational explanation for why U.S. advisers rely on
teaching and persuasion even when recipient incentives favor military weakness.

**Novelty implication:** The present paper is conditional on assistance changing
the targeted military function. It does not compete with an influence-centered
explanation of whether political reform occurs.

### Matisek 2018

**Citation:** Jahara Matisek. "The Crisis of American Military Assistance:
Strategic Dithering and Faberge Egg Armies." *Defense & Security Analysis*
34(3): 267-290.

**DOI:** `10.1080/14751798.2018.1500757`

**Use:** Fragility of externally supported militaries and the distinction
between apparent capability and durable institutional strength.

### Knowles and Matisek 2019

**Citation:** Emily Knowles and Jahara Matisek. "Western Security Force
Assistance in Weak States: Time for a Peacebuilding Approach." *The RUSI
Journal* 164(3): 10-21.

**DOI:** `10.1080/03071847.2019.1643258`

**Use:** Political and institutional foundations of sustainable security-force
development.

### Harkness 2022

**Citation:** Kristen A. Harkness. "Security Force Assistance to Cameroon: How
Building Enclave Units Deepens Autocracy." *International Affairs* 98(6):
2099-2117.

**DOI:** `10.1093/ia/iiac215`

**Use:** Demonstrates that tactically effective assisted units can produce
broader political consequences and that successful SFA can endogenously change
recipient political dynamics.

### Sandnes 2024

**Citation:** Marie Sandnes. "The Effect of Asymmetric Interdependence on the
Outcomes of Military Cooperation in the Sahel." *Cooperation and Conflict*
59(3): 405-424.

**DOI:** `10.1177/00108367231184717`

**Use:** Ownership, autonomy, and asymmetric interdependence in external
military cooperation.

### Rolandsen et al. 2026

**Citation:** Oystein H. Rolandsen et al. "Security Assistance and the Crisis of
the Liberal International Order." *International Studies Review* 28(2):
viag003.

**DOI:** `10.1093/isr/viag003`

**Use:** Current field-level positioning. The 2026 forum explicitly emphasizes
relational effects, unintended consequences, fragmentation, dependency, and
dissonance between assistance intentions and outcomes.

**Novelty implication:** A generic claim that security assistance produces
dependency would be insufficiently novel. The paper must specify the moving
production-constraint mechanism.

### U.S. Government Accountability Office 2023

**Citation:** *Building Partner Capacity: DOD and State Should Strengthen
Planning for Train and Equip Projects*, GAO-23-105842.

**Use:** Policy evidence that sustainment and absorptive-capacity planning remain
practical problems. GAO found that 42 of 46 reviewed Section 333 proposals did
not fully document sustainment, absorptive capacity, or measurable objectives.

**Current-status note:** GAO's public recommendation tracker reports that DOD
subsequently improved proposal guidance, while GAO continued monitoring whether
congressional notifications adequately documented absorptive capacity as of
2026. The manuscript should cite the underlying 2023 report for the reviewed
sample and avoid presenting the 2023 planning deficiency as an unchanged 2026
condition.

## Positioning statement for the manuscript

The literature already explains many reasons security assistance can fail to
produce effective, legitimate, or autonomous security forces. The manuscript
asks a different conditional question: **what happens to autonomy when
assistance successfully changes the military production function it targets?**

The answer proposed and tested here is that successful assistance can alter the
partner's supported operating envelope and cause the binding service constraint
to migrate. Autonomy then depends on whether indigenous production keeps pace
with demand at the service that binds the adapted system.
