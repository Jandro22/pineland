# Mali historical-validation memo v1

Status: **PRELIMINARY CODING UNDER FROZEN PROTOCOL**

Case: Malian Armed Forces, with primary attention to EUTM Mali's early GTIA
training effort, logistics and sustainment, and command and control between 2013
and 2015.

## Source hierarchy used in this memo

1. Michael Shurkin, Stephanie Pezard, and S. Rebecca Zimmerman, *Mali's Next
   Battle: Improving Counterterrorism Capabilities*, RAND Corporation,
   RR-1241. The report draws on interviews with Malian officers, EUTM personnel,
   MINUSMA personnel, and other advisers conducted in Mali in 2015.
2. European External Action Service material describing EUTM Mali's mandate,
   training role, advisory role, and stated objective of helping create
   self-sustainable Malian Armed Forces.

The RAND study is a research-institution source rather than an oversight audit.
Its interview evidence is therefore used cautiously, particularly where a
claim rests on retrospective assessments by advisers or Malian officers. EUTM
material establishes mission design and stated objectives but is not treated as
independent evidence of mission effectiveness.

## Episode A: EUTM GTIA regeneration and deployment, approximately 2013-2015

### Initial candidate bottleneck

**Multiple weaknesses, with force generation and basic unit capability prominent
after the 2012 collapse. Evidence strength: 2, moderate.**

EUTM's early effort focused on rebuilding usable maneuver formations through
the GTIA model. RAND reports that EUTM assembled personnel from existing
regiments into battalion-sized task forces, issued equipment, and subjected the
units to a 12-week training cycle emphasizing basic infantry skills and
specialized functions. By early 2015, six GTIAs had been trained, with eight
planned under the mandate.

The initial constraint should not be coded as force generation alone. RAND's
interviews and operational discussion also identify serious command,
coordination, logistics, maintenance, and institutional weaknesses that existed
during the same period. This makes Mali a deliberately difficult test of a
clean sequential-bottleneck claim.

### Assistance type

**Developmental, with limited material support and advisory assistance.**

EUTM supplied training, education, and institutional advice rather than acting
as the principal external combat force for the trained GTIAs. Its official
mandate describes the goal as restoring Malian military capacity and supporting
a self-sustainable force. The early mission concentrated on GTIA training while
the Advisory Task Force worked on broader reform.

### Targeted function improved

**Partial.**

RAND assesses the GTIAs as having improved the raw skills of participating
soldiers. The mission also introduced commander courses, train-the-trainer
courses, and specialized instruction. The same assessment cautions that the
training remained concentrated on basic infantry skills rather than the
collective and combined-arms competence needed for complex battalion operations.

The May 2014 Kidal offensive is important disconfirming evidence. An
EUTM-trained GTIA participated, but the operation revealed serious failures in
planning, coordination, sustainment, and the ability to combine elements from
different units. The case therefore supports a claim of **local improvement**,
not a claim that EUTM had solved the FAMa's broader operational-capability
problem.

### Operating demand expanded

**Partial to yes.**

The trained GTIAs became leading maneuver formations and rotated into active
service in northern Mali. This exposed them to long-distance operations in a
large, austere theater where sustainment, maintenance, command, communications,
and coordinated operations mattered more than they did during short training
cycles.

The evidence does not permit a precise estimate of how much demand was induced
by EUTM success itself rather than by Mali's pre-existing mission in the north.
The stronger claim is that improved and newly organized units were employed in
an operating environment whose service requirements exceeded what basic
tactical training alone could supply.

### Post-relief bottleneck

**Logistics, maintenance, and command and control. Evidence strength: 3 for
logistics and sustainment, 2 to 3 for command and coordination.**

RAND describes sustainment as a central weakness across the force. Logistics
and maintenance were repeatedly identified by both Malian officers and
international advisers as drivers of poor performance. Much equipment was not
serviceable, including most aircraft. The report also records the failure of
Malian logistics to sustain a previously U.S.-trained special-forces unit in the
remote desert and notes that even EUTM-trained GTIAs struggled to be equipped.

Command and control was also a major limitation. RAND's discussion of Kidal
emphasizes an inability to coordinate a hybrid force and notes that EUTM's
training had not prepared GTIAs for coordinated battalion-level operations.

### Bottleneck migration

**Unclear to partial, not a clean affirmative case.**

The observed sequence resembles the theory in one respect: an assistance effort
that improved personnel and basic unit skills did not translate into
whole-system operational capability because sustainment and command remained
limiting. However, the strongest rival interpretation is that these constraints
did not newly emerge after training. They were already severe weaknesses in the
Malian military.

Mali therefore should not be used as evidence that assistance *caused* a
force-generation-to-logistics migration. It is better evidence for the narrower
local-system divergence claim: improving a trained maneuver subsystem can
produce limited system yield when complementary institutions remain weak.

### Indigenous replacement

**Mixed and incomplete during the coded period.**

EUTM pursued train-the-trainer programs and advisory work intended to embed
skills and institutional practices in the Malian force. RAND nevertheless found
that turning doctrine into functioning sustainment systems remained difficult.
Its discussion of maintenance describes the problem partly as an organizational
and cultural deficit, not simply an equipment shortage.

The evidence therefore supports indigenous learning at the tactical level but
does not show comprehensive indigenous replacement of the institutional
functions needed to sustain complex operations.

### Withdrawal or support shock

**No clean shock in the 2013-2015 episode.**

EUTM remained present, other international actors operated simultaneously, and
Mali's security environment changed substantially. Later reductions and changes
in international involvement were intertwined with coups, relations with
external partners, the changing insurgency, and the presence or departure of
other foreign forces. Those later events are not treated here as a clean
withdrawal test of the early GTIA episode.

## Rival explanations and disconfirming evidence

Mali poses several major challenges to the production-mechanism interpretation:

- logistics and command weaknesses clearly predated or accompanied the EUTM
  training effort, weakening a strict migration claim;
- the Kidal operation involved a hybrid force assembled from EUTM-trained and
  other elements, creating a coordination problem that cannot be attributed to
  training breadth alone;
- Malian strategic decision-making and planning contributed directly to the
  Kidal failure;
- corruption, personnel management, ethnic and political tensions, and weak
  governance affected the force independently of the modeled production
  mechanism;
- the adversary, terrain, geographic scale, and threat environment created
  demands that were not produced by assistance itself;
- EUTM officers acknowledged that their early program did not aim to train
  battalion-level coordinated operations, so some observed gaps were outside
  the intervention's initial scope rather than newly generated failures.

These factors make Mali a useful hard case. The theory should survive only in a
narrow form: local tactical development cannot substitute for complementary
system capacity.

## Current interpretation

Mali is intentionally **not** a clean success for the theory. It supports the
claim that training and formation-level improvement can be stranded behind
complementary logistics, maintenance, and command weaknesses. It provides much
weaker evidence that assistance itself caused the binding constraint to migrate,
because the later constraints were already visible. In the final paper, this
case should function as a scope-condition and disconfirmation check rather than
as another affirmative migration example.
