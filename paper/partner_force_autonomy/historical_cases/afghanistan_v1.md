# Afghanistan historical-validation memo v1

Status: **SOURCE-AUDITED CODING UNDER FROZEN PROTOCOL**

Case: Afghan National Defense and Security Forces, with primary attention to
Afghan Air Force maintenance and the logistics dependence of conventional
ground forces.

## Source hierarchy used in this memo

1. SIGAR, *Why the Afghan Security Forces Collapsed*, interim report
   SIGAR-22-22-IP and final report SIGAR-23-16-IP.
2. SIGAR, *Afghan Air Forces: DOD Has Taken Steps to Develop Afghan Aviation
   Capability but Continued Contractor Support is Needed to Sustain Aircraft*,
   SIGAR-21-14-AR.
3. SIGAR April 30, 2021 Quarterly Report to Congress, including TAAC-Air
   maintenance assessments.

These are oversight sources. Statements attributed to former Afghan commanders
inside SIGAR reports are treated as interview evidence rather than independent
measurements.

## Episode A: aviation maintenance and contractor logistics support

### Initial candidate bottleneck

**Maintenance and aviation logistics. Evidence strength: 3, strong.**

SIGAR reported that the Afghan Air Force and Special Mission Wing lacked the
capability to maintain their fleets without contracted logistics support. In
2020, Afghan maintainers performed at most 40 percent of maintenance on most
AAF airframes, with the major exception of the Mi-17 fleet, where they performed
95 percent. SIGAR also reported that TAAC-Air assessed in January 2021 that no
AAF airframe could remain combat effective for more than a few months without
continued contractor support.

### Assistance type

**Hybrid, with a large substitutive component.**

The United States funded contractor maintenance while also training Afghan
maintainers. Contractor support therefore supplied service directly while the
development effort attempted to increase indigenous maintenance capacity.

### Targeted function improved

**Yes.**

Contractor support sustained fleet readiness and enabled Afghan aviation to
provide air support, transport, resupply, and medical evacuation. Indigenous
maintainer qualification also improved unevenly over time.

### Operating demand expanded

**Yes, with strong qualitative support.**

SIGAR reports that Taliban pressure on ground supply routes pushed the ANDSF to
move personnel and materiel by air. Conventional ground forces therefore relied
on an aviation system whose operational role expanded as ground logistics became
more difficult.

### Constraint migration or persistence

**Persistent aviation-maintenance and logistics constraint rather than clear
migration away from it.**

The strongest evidence is not that maintenance was fully relieved and another
constraint replaced it. The evidence indicates that external substitution kept
the aviation-maintenance constraint from binding operational output while the
underlying indigenous maintenance deficit remained.

This pattern resembles the Stage-4 matched-logistics result more closely than
the command-to-logistics migration result.

### Indigenous replacement

**Negative or incomplete.**

SIGAR reported substantial variation by airframe. Afghan personnel were capable
of performing most Mi-17 maintenance, but much less maintenance on several U.S.
platforms. The transition from Mi-17s to UH-60s increased the sophistication of
the supported fleet and, according to SIGAR's reporting of TAAC-Air estimates,
moved the expected date of AAF self-sufficiency to at least 2030.

This is directly relevant to the paper's denominator logic. A more capable
supported fleet can increase the service requirement faster than indigenous
maintenance production matures.

### Withdrawal or support shock

**Present.**

Contractor withdrawal sharply reduced maintenance capacity. SIGAR reports that
former Afghan commander Sami Sadat stated that 60 percent of Black Hawks were
grounded within months and that battle-damaged or maintenance-needing aircraft
were grounded after contractors departed. SIGAR links reduced aviation support
to worsening resupply and medical-evacuation problems for isolated ground
forces.

### Observed post-shock response

**Severe degradation followed by broader force collapse.**

The aviation and logistics evidence supports a service-production mechanism,
but it should not be treated as a sufficient explanation for the collapse of
the ANDSF as a whole.

## Rival explanations and disconfirming evidence

SIGAR's final collapse report identifies multiple short-term and systemic
factors beyond logistics and maintenance, including:

- the U.S. decision and timetable for withdrawal;
- severe morale effects following the U.S.-Taliban agreement;
- changes in the level of U.S. military support;
- corruption;
- frequent leadership changes and political appointments;
- failure to implement a coherent national security strategy;
- Taliban exploitation of ANDSF weaknesses;
- long-running problems in training, force design, oversight, and police
  development.

These factors materially limit any claim that a moving production bottleneck
"explained" Afghanistan's collapse.

## Current interpretation

Afghanistan is strong evidence for the **substitution and indigenous-replacement
portion** of the theory. It is weaker as clean evidence of bottleneck migration
because maintenance and logistics never appear to have been autonomously solved.
The case is especially useful for showing that high supported operational output
can coexist with a service system that remains externally produced.
