# Historical source audit v1

Status: **COMPLETE FOR DRAFT 0.6 HISTORICAL PROCESS CLAIMS**

Date: 2026-09-21

This audit verifies the claims used in the manuscript's four-case historical
process section against the frozen source hierarchy. It does not turn the case
evidence into a causal estimate. Claims that cannot meet the sequence burden are
retained as partial or adverse evidence.

## Afghanistan

### Verified primary claims

- SIGAR 21-14-AR, the January 2022 unclassified version of a classified report
  issued in January 2021, states that the AAF and SMW continued to depend on
  contractor logistics support and that neither advisory organization believed
  the Afghan air forces could sustain themselves without continued advisory and
  contracted-logistics assistance.
- The same report records continuing progress in combat capabilities while
  identifying sustainability gaps in personnel, training, support positions,
  leadership, and contractor-dependent maintenance. This supports the
  supported-capability versus indigenous-retention distinction without implying
  that aviation was institutionally healthy.
- SIGAR's collapse reports document that Afghan maintainers performed at most
  about 40 percent of maintenance on most AAF airframes around 2020, with the
  Mi-17 as a major exception, and that the shift from Mi-17s to UH-60s pushed
  expected self-sufficiency further into the future.
- SIGAR records a severe contractor-withdrawal shock, including former Afghan
  commanders' reports that many UH-60s were grounded after contractor departure.
  SIGAR also documents the growing importance of air resupply and medical
  evacuation as Taliban pressure disrupted ground supply routes.

### Retained qualification

The collapse report identifies numerous coexisting causes, including withdrawal
politics and timing, morale, leadership, corruption, strategic failure, and
Taliban adaptation. Afghanistan is therefore coded **strong for external
substitution and weak retention, partial for constraint migration, and not a
single-mechanism explanation of the 2021 collapse**.

## Iraq

### Verified primary claims

- GAO-07-582T reports that DOD counted about 323,000 trained and equipped MOD
  and MOI personnel in November 2006 while simultaneously identifying logistics
  and sustainment planning and execution as the most serious capability
  shortcoming and warning that the ISF would require continued Coalition support
  without a developed logistics system.
- The same audit records national and regional logistics centers lagging behind
  lower-echelon formations, shortages of trained mechanics and logisticians,
  maintenance and stockage problems, and Coalition provision of warehouse,
  transport, maintenance, and other support.
- GAO-08-143R reports growth from more than 171,000 trained and equipped ISF
  personnel in July 2005 to about 359,600 by September 2007 while support
  capabilities remained immature. It explicitly cautions against interpreting
  unit-level 'independence' without accounting for Coalition enablers and
  support.

### Retained qualification

The evidence is unusually strong for a force-generation-to-sustainment sequence,
but sectarianism, command and control, personnel accounting, violence, political
incentives, and corruption also shaped performance. The coded episode ends in
2008 and is **not used as a monocausal explanation of the 2014 collapse**.

## Mali

### Verified primary and high-quality research claims

- EU material confirms that EUTM Mali was launched in February 2013 to provide
  training, education, and advice and explicitly framed the mission around a
  self-sustainable Malian Armed Forces rather than direct EU combat substitution.
- RAND RR-1241 reports a 12-week EUTM training cycle centered primarily on basic
  infantry skills and describes meaningful value from those contributions.
- RAND also documents persistent logistics, maintenance, coordination, and
  collective-operation weaknesses. Its discussion of the May 2014 Kidal defeat
  identifies coordination and logistical failures and records EUTM officers'
  observation that their program did not train GTIAs to conduct coordinated
  battalion-level operations.

### Retained qualification

The complementary weaknesses were already visible before or during the training
effort. Mali therefore **does not establish endogenous constraint migration**.
It remains a mixed/hard case supporting local-system divergence and the scope
condition that targeted tactical improvement can be stranded behind pre-existing
system weaknesses.

## Colombia

### Verified primary claims

- GAO-03-783 documents early shortages in qualified pilots and mechanics,
  multi-year mechanic qualification timelines, under-strength aviation staffing,
  and logistics/maintenance funding below requested levels while the Army
  aviation mission supported nationwide counternarcotics and counterinsurgency
  operations.
- GAO-09-71 documents an integrated U.S. support architecture including
  infrastructure, maintenance, contract pilots and mechanics, pilot and mechanic
  training, safety and operating standards, and logistics. As of June 2008, 43
  contract pilots and 87 contract mechanics were still required for the Plan
  Colombia Helicopter Program.
- GAO-09-71 also documents an explicit contractor-reduction and nationalization
  plan intended to replace external pilots and mechanics with Colombian Army
  personnel and transfer parts, supplies, aircraft, and other recurring
  responsibilities.
- GAO-19-106 reports that Colombia began nationalizing 62 Army aircraft in 2008
  and assumed full responsibility for their maintenance and operations in 2012.
- GAO-13-674 supplies important adverse evidence: the related Regional
  Helicopter Training Center's full nationalization was pushed to 2018 because
  Colombia was not yet ready to assume full financial responsibility, and
  maintenance, parts, tools, simulators, and training had different transfer
  schedules.

### Retained qualification

Colombia is coded **strong for phased indigenous replacement and retention,
partial for discrete bottleneck migration**. Stronger institutions, domestic
resources, political commitment, long duration, and broader military changes
are rival or enabling explanations for the wider Colombian outcome.

## Cross-case audit result

The four cases survive source audit only in differentiated roles:

| Case | Audited use in paper | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| Afghanistan | External substitution, weak indigenous replacement, withdrawal sensitivity | Strong for retention mechanism; partial for migration |
| Iraq | Sequential expansion of fielded force followed by lagging logistics/sustainment | Strongest migration analogue; politically confounded |
| Mali | Targeted training gains stranded behind broader weaknesses | Mixed/adverse for clean migration; useful scope-condition case |
| Colombia | Phased replacement and successful transfer of major aviation responsibilities | Strong positive retention case; partial for migration |

No case is used to estimate the frequency, magnitude, or population-average
effect of the model mechanism.
