## Episode A: force generation outpaces sustainment, 2005-2008

### Initial candidate bottleneck

The early assistance program strongly emphasized **force generation, training,
and equipment**. By November 2006, DOD reported roughly 323,000 trained and
equipped personnel across the Ministries of Defense and Interior.

The relevant dynamic is that this apparent progress in force generation did not
imply self-sustaining operational capacity.

### Targeted relief

**Force generation improved substantially.**

The United States and Coalition created large numbers of trained and equipped
personnel and fielded Iraqi units. GAO reported multiple Iraqi Army divisions,
brigades, and battalions assuming lead responsibility for security missions.

### Operating demand expanded

**Yes.**

Larger fielded forces and expanded lead responsibility created continuing
requirements for fuel, ammunition, vehicle maintenance, warehousing,
transportation, supply-chain management, and command support.

### Post-relief bottleneck

**Logistics and sustainment. Evidence strength: 3, strong.**

GAO reported that DOD regarded logistics and sustainment planning and execution
as the most serious capability shortcoming for both the Ministry of Defense and
Ministry of Interior. GAO also found that national and regional logistics
centers lagged behind the creation of lower-echelon formations and that this lag
made Ministry of Defense self-sufficiency problematic.

The Ministry of Interior remained dependent on Coalition support to operate its
warehouse system. Vehicle maintenance was constrained by the diversity and
technical complexity of the fleet and by shortages of trained mechanics.

### Bottleneck migration

**Supported.**

The sequence is consistent with force generation ceasing to be the sole or
dominant observable constraint while logistics and sustainment became central
limits on independent execution. This is not a clean controlled transition,
but the temporal ordering is unusually explicit in the oversight record.

### Indigenous replacement

**Incomplete during the coded period.**

GAO reported continuing Coalition support for key logistics functions and later
warned that Iraqi units classified as capable of leading operations still
required external enabling support. A 2007 independent assessment cited by GAO
similarly concluded that the ISF would continue relying on Coalition combat
support, combat-service support, and training.

### Assistance architecture

**Initially substitution-heavy, with later developmental correction.**

Coalition forces supplied equipment, fuel, ammunition, warehousing support, and
other enabling services while also attempting to build Iraqi logistics
institutions. The oversight record shows that logistics development followed or
lagged the rapid generation of operational units.

### Retrospective institutional lesson

The DoD Inspector General's 2015 cross-case lessons-learned review explicitly
recommended developing logistics and sustainment **concurrently with the buildup
of operational forces**, building demand-driven supply systems, and limiting
U.S. and Coalition push resupply.

That lesson maps closely to the theory's production logic without proving the
theory by itself.

## Rival explanations and disconfirming evidence

Iraq is not a pure logistics case. GAO and the wider literature document other
major constraints:

- sectarian and militia influence;
- absenteeism and inaccurate personnel accounting;
- command and control weaknesses;
- intelligence shortfalls;
- corruption and procurement problems;
- recipient-provider political interest divergence.

The later 2014 collapse of major Iraqi Army formations therefore cannot be
attributed to logistics alone. A stronger version of this case will need to
separate the 2005-2008 institution-building episode from the political and
military processes leading to 2014.

## Current interpretation

Iraq is the strongest preliminary historical analogue for **bottleneck
migration**. The oversight record explicitly juxtaposes a rapidly generated,
trained, and equipped force with lagging logistics and sustainment institutions.
The case is most useful if the manuscript limits the claim to that production
sequence rather than attempting to explain the total effectiveness of the Iraqi
military or the 2014 collapse with one mechanism.
